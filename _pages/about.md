I develop experimental approaches to understand molecular and cellular functions. My current work connects genetic variation with disease  phenotypes via large-scale single-cell phenotyping. The goal is to understand the disease mechanisms underlying genetic cardiomyopathies, to design specific molecular therapies.

As a Research Scientist in the Steinmetz group at EMBL Heidelberg I develop scalable experimental and computational approaches to understand how genetic variants alter molecular and cellular phenotypes, with a particular focus on cardiovascular disease.
My current research focuses on RBM20-associated dilated cardiomyopathy. By combining saturation mutagenesis with image enabled cell sorting, I aim to build variant-to-function maps that reveal how individual mutations perturb cellular biology. This work helped to classify thousands of possible missense variants into distinct pathogenic groups. We identified variants that either lost their native protein function or that just mislocalized with an intact native protein function after a mutation occurred. This is important to design tailored precision variant specific therapies, because it shows that a single drug for a single genetic disease approach is not achievable. The principle of physically separating two populations and then apply stringent computational approaches to identify underlying biology is something I learned during my PhD. Under supervision of Günter Kramer and Bernd Bukau, I studied in a similar way, how protein complexes assemble while their subunits are still being synthesized. This work helped to establish co-translational assembly as a widespread mechanism of protein-complex biogenesis. This is important because a newly made protein faces a surprisingly difficult problem: how does it find the correct partner among millions of molecules in a crowded cell? One solution is not to search at all. We found that many proteins begin assembling while they are still being synthesized by neighboring ribosomes, allowing the correct partners to meet before entering the crowded cytoplasm.

Across these projects, I am particularly interested in combining method development, large-scale experimentation and quantitative data analysis to answer biological questions that are difficult to address one variant or one protein at a time.
